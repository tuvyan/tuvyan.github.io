TUVYAN

Nostalgia in Eric Gamalinda's My Sad Republic

 Scott Magkachi Sabóy

[Note: This was a paper I submitted to Prof. Delfin Lindain Tolentino in 2009 for his Literary Criticism class, one of the subjects in the Master of Arts in Language and Literature at the University of the Philippines Baguio]


“To be ignorant of what happened before you were bornis to remain a child forever.” Cicero

This article explores the theme of nostalgia that pervades Eric Gamalinda’s My Sad Republic, a novel in English that won the Philippine Centennial Literary Prize (1998). Set against the backdrop of the Philippine Revolution and the Philippine-American War at the twilight of the 19th century and the dawn of the 20th, this historical novel is a fictionalized account of the exploits of a great revolutionary-faith healer in Negros Island named Dionisio Magbuela (Seguela or Barlucia in some historical accounts), more popularly known as “Papa Isio.”

A Tapestry of Nostalgia

The novel is an intricately woven literary cloth, as exemplified by these details: A salvaged pirate’s locket containing a human nail that continues to grow like a chambered nautilus. Pigafetta’s mirror which recalls of the beginnings of colonialism but reveals the end of the world. Santa Regina’s bathwater a drink from which promises Lethe’s power but punishes one with delirium or disorientation. Florinda and Yñigo’s cameo that brings in a remembrance of a lost past, a memento of one’s obscure roots. Isio’s bracelet of leaves that is supposed to be a sturdy token of love but which disintegrates in the end. Doña Madrigal’s trinket which she claims reminds her not of a once-upon-a-time blossoming love, but simply of her possession of a heart. Books that can take the reader to wonderful worlds real or imagined, past or present but can make one painfully aware of the ignominy of the present. The shamans’ (and shawomen’s, if you please) dark arts issuing from the depths of an animistic past that compete with the theological light coming from a Western religion and the blessings of modern medicine. America’s constitution that, while foreign in origin and difficult to understand, nevertheless inspires one to carve an indigenous government out of a larger territory now claimed by another power from abroad. A land’s struggle to keep the beauty of its rustic existence while being forced to accommodate the sophistications of urban life.

One bright thread runs through this whole literary tapestry: nostalgia; a hankering for home, for a bygone era, for the good, old days. A sense of naufragio – shipwreckness, lostness – pervades the whole story: a lost dream, lost republic, lost inheritance, lost ancestors, lost son, lost love, lost sight, lost identity... It is not just a lost past that makes these experiences nostalgic, however; it is largely formed by the fact that a lost past holds no possibility of resurrection. As Linda Hutcheon (1998) perfectly puts it, nostalgia depends “precisely on the irrecoverable nature of the past for its emotional impact and appeal. It is the very pastness of the past, its inaccessibility, that likely accounts for a large part of nostalgia’s power.”

Intertwined with this are other sets of threads that crisscross the breadth and length of this literary cloth. One is the thread of sadness dramatized, among others, in the “dolorous rains,” the “benevolent sadness” on Isio’s countenance, the “sorrowful mysteries.” Then there are threads of memory: multifaceted in form – it comes in one’s sleeping and waking hours, appears on familiar and unfamiliar faces, springs from likely and unlikely places, mixes fact and fiction; too tenacious to be forgotten – the scars remain, the rituals persist, the roads roll on, the land lives on; and cruel in one’s solitude – free in one’s prison cell, open-eyed in one’s blindness, fresh in one’s fading years. There are also the threads of change – a change of masters, residences, tools, perspectives, plans, statuses, lifestyles, and what have you.

But paradoxically, all these changes do not make substantial changes – only superficial ones as illustrated by a peasant (Isio) momentarily slipping on borrowed leather shoes on his splayed feet and changing his rough working clothes with cotton and silk lent by the dowager he serves. This reminds us of that French adage: Plus ca change, plus c’est la meme chose (The more things change, the more they stay the same). The quote may well have originally referred to governments, but it surely finds its home within the various scenes on the pages of this novel.

And here, taking cue from the novel’s persistent rains, we can change the metaphor to say that all these threads of change can be viewed as being transformed into streams that converge to become “a roaring current of change…that…overturns institutions, shifts… values and shrivels… roots” (Toffler 1970, 1)

Like Toffer’s Future Shock, the changes mightily pour down on the novel’s characters forcing them to swim with the current. The current washes onto the banks of their culture a new tongue, horseless carriages, shops, schools and theaters which they are compelled to use or go to (Gamalinda 2000, 372-386).

Centennial and Millenial

What could have been a more perfect theme than this for a centennial celebration that rolled into a millennial transition? For as Hutcheon observed, “Nostalgia, is given surplus meaning and value at… millennial moments” (1998). The 100-year anniversary of the Philippine Republic takes us to our roots just as the beginning of another thousand years sets our sights on to our future.

The backward look presents us with a saddening realization of how much we have lost and that our struggles today seem to be largely a replay of our struggles before, though in a different form (cf. Groyon 2001; San Juan 1998). On the other hand, the forward look presents us with a gargantuan task especially in view of our bid for a higher level of industrialization in this new millennium, which threatens to sever us from our roots. For as Bienvenido Lumbera (2005) warns, “There is danger… that in the rush to NIChood, the country might be shedding his cultural identity, perceiving this as disposable cultural baggage to be exchanged for material prosperity.”

So this dual outlook reveals our dilemma as a nation on how to move forward without being tied to the past, or how to carry the past as we journey on, or what provision to get from yesterday for the needs of tomorrow.

How then should this problem be dealt with? Or perhaps the question should be, How have we dealt with this problem? Gamalinda’s novel gives us glimpses of the romantic and pragmatic ways we have attempted to address this conundrum.

The Romantic View1

One way this view is expressed is in the creation of or search for a means of escape from reality. This could be symbolized by a secret room in a labyrinthine house that becomes one’s familiar hideaway (Gamalinda 2000,43), a trysting place along the seashore where two lovers can be alone and unmolested (ibid., 47, 76), books that could transform a hellish house into a heavenly abode – at least in one’s imagination (ibid., 156), and a cathedral whose yawning hall and suffering saints can give temporary peace and company (ibid., 74).

Escape comes in the form of entertainment like those tear-jerking operas (ibid., 53, ff.) that awaken supposedly deadened sensibilities and that lift spirits to emotional highs; orgiastic pursuits like those ceaseless copulations (ibid., 357) that replace cries of suffering with cries of ecstasy; daydreaming periods which affords a release from a cloistered existence and to an other-worldly existence where fresh kisses turn beasts into princes and worlds are transformed according to the wishes of the heart (ibid., 247, 184).

But repression, denial and fantasy as coping mechanisms for nostalgia have never been successful in preventing repressed memories from eventually finding their outlets (ibid., 251,252) or in insulating us from the harsh realities that envelop us. These can only “forever haunt us with [their] magic” (Jose 1998, 197).

Toward the end of the novel (p. 373, ff.), Papa Isio’s last apocalyptic vision was preceded by widespread deaths of animal life and the poisoning of waters around his mountain camp. Thinking that it was a heaven-wrought scourge and sign that demanded penance “to atone for their sins” against their forefathers whose legacy they had forsaken, he urged a return to the old ways. Primarily, it meant returning to the language and religion of pre-colonial times – a radical proposal that borders on escapism but which seemed to offer a workable solution.

Give up Spanish, give up anything in the language that broke our hearts. Do not be tempted by English, the language that breaks our souls. Speak only in the tongue of tongues, the speech before any white man came. Believe only in the spirits exiled by the Christian gods, the souls of the dead and the souls of trees and rocks and rain. (Gamalinda 2000, 372)

Discarding the language of the colonizers is perfectly understandable. For one, the language of Spain may be beautiful “but it tastes bitter on our tongues” (ibid., 227) as it reminds us of three centuries of oppression much like the bitter herb of the Passover reminding the Jews of their four centuries of slavery in Egypt. Two, the language of the United States has been the Americans’ tool of control over the archipelago. The imposition of English upon us both educated and miseducated us (Constantino 1982; cf. Gamalinda 2003). It serves as a mark of enslavement not unlike the pierced ear of a Greek slave. But we can only wish scraping the language of our colonizers off our consciousness and speech will not leave us scarred and bleeding.

Discarding the religion of the colonizers seems to be a noble act. Indeed, would it not be wonderful to return to that time when the pale-skinned gold and glory seekers did not yet permanently anchor their ships on our shores: “before the idols were burned, before God became Spanish, before God became American” (Gamalinda 2000, 330).

But it’s quite ironic and paradoxical that no matter how much we try to purge our religious lives from the White Man’s belief system, we have found ourselves continually incorporating aspects of that faith into our culture. So we have an Isio Magbuela who rejects the pontifications of the Vatican but who nevertheless appropriates for himself the title of the Roman Pontiff, who uses holy water and the cross to ward off the malevolent creatures spawned by his animistic (or anitistic?) religion, who utters the Dominus Vobiscum in his ancient rituals, and who dons the priestly vestments but does not put on the priestly shoes.

This split-level nature of our faith might explain our readiness to accept Messianic claims of an indigenized Moses (ibid., 170) or Jesus (see Dimayuga 1997). On the other hand, this is fuel to the White Man’s flame of evangelistic missions and of his messianic complex:

[Smith] was convinced they were Christian only in name, but their souls remained possessed by demonic origins…in his heart he could still not believe it was the Pope who saved him and his men from yellow fever. Not him: natural causes, the body heals itself, if the spirit is willing… But he had always been convinced he came to these islands with a greater purpose: to save the people from themselves. Therefore war. Therefore science and progress. How could that be wrong? (Gamalinda 2000, 337):

Interestingly, in another work Gamalinda (2003) makes the following comment that dovetails with the above statements:

The image of savior and redeemer was something the United States exploited with the precision and efficiency of a professional publicity agent since 1899, when it invaded the archipelago. Since then, there have been two major deities in the pantheon of the Filipino psyche: God and America.

Split-level Christianity may also explain our gullibility in seeing miracles in a clump of banana plants, the rocks in caves and the formation of the clouds. So whatever is spectacular is a miracle – “Everything was a miracle,” earthly life can be eternal –“We’ll never die…,” and the impossible is never impossible – “Anything was possible” (ibid., 145, 169, 170).

Papa Isio’s solution to the troubles plaguing his Republic sounded so sure and simple. But the persistence of the plague only served to highlight the naïvete of his plan. This seems to typify the danger of romanticizing a pre-colonial era so as to bring us at odds with reality. Historical facts are thus distorted and we can be lured into what Emil Jurado calls as a “screwed up nationalism” (2007). Maybe as screwed up as the identity of an anti-American Filipino with a Western-sounding name, wearing Western-design clothes, living under Western-patterned laws, and yes, wallowing in Western-born vices.

But to be fair, Papa Isio has not always stood on the clouds, for he planted his splayed feet on the ground as can be shown by his realization that anarchy should not be the final state, that an egalitarian state should be governed by the rule of moral and political law (Gamalinda 2000, 267).

Pragmatic View

Tomas Agustin’s capitulation to the new colonial masters, the onset of a fad for American-style clothes among the young, Martinez’s counterpoints to Papa Isio’s idealism, and Asuncion’s acceptance of the roles each one must play in the game show of life as well as her determination to make plants grow on her sanctified land all serve as representations of a pragmatic approach to the pervading nostalgia in the story.

To the pragmatist, resistance is fruitless and accommodation is the wiser course of action to take in the face of a superior force. The new language must be learned, peace must reign, and sights must be set on the greener pastures of the new colonial master:

How lovely to learn a new language, so different form the Spanish we all know. Even the money is different. Pennies and dollars and nickels. And dime, what a lovely word. Like diamond. Do you have lots of diamonds. What does America look like. Buildings and bridges and beauty and blessed bounty. I must go there some time, when all this business of war is done…

 

She liked to say the names of the cities the colonel had mentioned to her. San Francisco and Chicago and New York. Better than the names of saints. And best of all: Mississippi. Mellifluous. Say it again and again, water on your tongue. (Ibid., 243, 247; cf. 342,346,348)

Further, reason must reign supreme over superstition and the force of determination should disprove fatalism. City medicines must replace the shaman’s “barks and potions” and Easter Egg Hunt must replace the usual rustic games.

Behind this pragmatism though is the insecurity created by a borrowed, artificial character and the sacrifice of one’s own flesh and blood. Viewed thus, the line that divides pragmatism and idealism becomes blurred and we come full circle to where we began.

Final Note

Gamalinda’s “remake” of Papa Isio and the circumstances surrounding his life could indeed “pique Negrosanons and historians” as one of the novel’s reviewers put it (Groyon 2007). However, it may well be that just as Gamalinda himself found in this little known Filipino patriot’s life “a parallel to the obscurity of the Filipino-American War…that nonetheless changed the destinies of the people of the Philippines and the United States,” (Gamalinda 2000, 391), so can we find in his novel a fine dramatization of our distorted understanding or hazy view of our own history and, consequently, a dramatization of our national identity crisis.

Gamalinda’s dramatis personae offer us a peek into our collective consciousness and the possible ways we can deal with our inner turmoil. Maybe, we can learn from the determination of a Papa Isio who battles all odds in pursuit of a vision. Or from the skepticism of a Tomas Agustin and the sporadic pragmatism of a Martinez. Or the decision of an Asuncion to break the ground no matter how sacred it is and to plant anew or to plant new crops.

Then can we weave a new tapestry of old and new threads with a bright thread of hope and triumph running all over it. A tapestry that showcases a dream, one once voiced by a one-time idealist whose tyranny had shattered that same dream:

Today, many Filipinos dream the same dreams – to create with dignity a new country, a new people, and to attain the greatness that was once ours; to return to every individual decency, dignity, and hope instead of frustration, deficiency, and defeat. And in lieu of failure, once in a while, success. (Marcos 1980, 58)

Along with this dream should be an ardent desire to listen to the stories of our past and be inspired to carry on the unfinished task of nation-building. As Angela Stuart-Santiago (2007) articulated:

Every island, every town, every tribe must have its stories to tell of the pain and shame of that disgraceful passage from Spanish colonization to Americanization. Stories that bear telling and retelling, the sooner to dispel the clouds obscuring that critical turn in our history, the sooner to confront ourselves and learn from our miserable mistakes.

 

Until then, in our landscape of despair, we will continue to believe in miracles, the quick if wondrous fix, a la EDSA.
