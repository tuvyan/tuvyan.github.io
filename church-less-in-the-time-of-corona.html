Church-less in the Time of 'Corona'

Church-less in the Time of 'Corona'

Originally posted on 12 April 2020

CHURCHRELIGIONCHRISTIANITYFUNDAMENTALISMHEALTH

Scott Magkachi Saboy

5/2/20245 min read

The coronavirus has now firmly stuck its tentacles all over the globe so much so that even church people have been forced to hunker down in their houses on Sundays.

Well, save for some would-be Darwin Awardees in America and elsewhere who believe that since they are "in Jesus" and therefore "covered by the blood of Jesus," they have nothing to fear and will continue to do their regular congregational services without any PPE.

Largely through sheer force of habit since childhood, many Christians have naturally associated “church” with the act of "congregating," "going to mass" or "attending church services" and a specific building. This calls to mind the Greek word ἐκκλησία (ekklesia), from which we get ecclesiology or ‘the study of the church’ and ecclesiastical ‘relating to the church.’ There's a bit of confusion here for etymologically, this Greek term and its cognates which exegetes say occur 188 times in the Judeao-Christian Bible originally referred to a group of people being "called out" or gathered in one place (i.e., an assembly -- see, for instance, Acts 9:3). Regrettably, the King James Version (KJV) scholars mistranslated the term as "church" 115x and properly translated it as "assembly" only 3x. Church actually comes from the Anglo-Saxon word circe or cirice (cf. Dan. kirke; Dutch kerk; Ger. kirche; Scot. kirk) which is said to have probably been derived from the Greek term κυριακὸν (kyriakon) 'of the Lord.' So ekklesia refers to people, not to buildings. (See online Greek lexicon here and here

Nevertheless, church is now commonly used to refer to both the worshipers and the house of worship. Many people have gotten so used to gathering in a building that their religious lives have become boundto or confined within their respective cathedrals, chapels, or worship houses. They being creatures of habit just like the rest of us, being absent in a congregational worship in a particular place has meant for them a lack of spirituality or a missing vital aspect of their religious life. For them, their Christian lives cannot be complete — or worse, their souls could be hell-bound — without the familiar weekly rituals of responsorial praying, congregational singing, sermon delivery, or partaking of the body and blood of Jesus, and yes, the annual Easter Egg Hunting or some other activities peculiar to their respective faith community — all done within the premises of a church property. Thus, come CoViD19 and high fever, many people still insist on "going to church."

Others have taken the idea to still higher heights by treating Christian places of worship -- even the fixtures inside the building -- as sacred. According to them, the church building and its premises are "holy ground" and thus governed by certain rules which often border on the superstitious and magical: can't eat in the church, can't do medical training or other "worldly" activities in church, can't wear slippers in church; divine presence is strongest at the altar, touching relics gives luck, and so on.

On the other hand, there are other Christians who understand that the nature of the church goes beyond physical structures. For after all, they point out, the anonymous writers of the Gospels of Matthew and John have Jesus saying, "Where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I in the midst of them" (Matt. 18:20), and "the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain nor yet at Jerusalem worship the Father... God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth" (John 4: 21, 24). They also point to a Pauline text which refers to the body of the believer as the "temple of the Holy Ghost" (I Cor. 6:19). For many of them, becoming a Christian enrolls one in the universal assembly of believers. Thus, they can "do church" online and belong to the church of Facebook and YouTube and/or "join worship" with a church halfway across the globe through their TVs, PCs, tablets, snd phones. So even during lockdowns, they could still be in sync with other worshipers anywhere, anytime. They might be alone at home, but they are one with the virtual community of faith worldwide. They might be out of the church building, but they are not churchless.

What about the so-called "unchurched," the "Nones" like me who no longer consider themselves members of any church and/or have no compulsion at all to go to church? I guess for some of us who still consider themselves believers, worshiping God is neither exclusive to group rituals nor confined to a particular space or an overtly religious activity. It operates mostly on an individual level, and can be done almost anywhere from a mining shaft underground or a ledge on a mountaintop, to a space station near the moon. So from this vantagepoint, whether one tends to endangered animals in a forest reserve far from civilization or treats patients in a medical facility at the heart of a metropolis, s/he still “performs” worship by serving Nature and humanity.


As for me, I have come to see that the church — notwithstanding its mystical claims — is just like any other human organization: it is a social institution that answers our need to belong and interact with others, to have a sense of security through numbers, to set and follow rules or values or goals, to create or adhere to a pattern of thinking and behavior, and to have a sense of purpose through a common cause.

Also, lockdown or not, Sundays or any "sacred" day is for me just like any other day -- a time to make myself useful, earn my keep, be of service to others, fulfil my familial and/or other secular duties, learn or hone a skill set, muse about life, rest and recharge, or do any such other mundane things.

For me, so-called sacred days are man-made creations — born out of a particular culture, and meant to serve a particular psychological or social purpose. Sanctity is not inherent to a particular day; it is merely a perspective arbitrarily imposed on a unit of time.

I do not regard any day as holy; I view each day as special. A day is not made special by mysticism; its mere occurrence makes it so. So then, to live another day is a privilege -- a gift, as it were, from an impersonal cosmos. This short life is all I've got, and I must make the best out of it. When it's my time to go, I'll be gone forever, and I'm not coming back. I do not care for living forever, for the universe and eternity are already compressed into my body and my lifetime. I am grateful to have lived at all, and feel lucky to have lived this long. If I die, and the bad or good memories I leave behind will be resurrected by those who knew me, I can only hope that they will remember me not only as one whose life was fraught with human frailties, but also one whose life was well-lived.

And so in moments such as this one, as all around me loved ones, friends, and strangers die as quickly as I type a word, I am one in thought with Harriet Beecher Stowe who, in Uncle Tom's Cabin, wrote:

"Death! Strange that there should be such a word, and such a thing, and we ever forget it; that one should be living, warm and beautiful, full of hopes, desires and wants, one day, and the next be gone, utterly gone, and forever!”

But like her, I continue to live with hope as well:

“The longest way must have its close - the gloomiest night will wear on to a morning.”

And so this Easter Sunday, life goes on with a purpose, existence continues to have meaning -- even for this devangelized, churchless one in the time of 'Corona.'
